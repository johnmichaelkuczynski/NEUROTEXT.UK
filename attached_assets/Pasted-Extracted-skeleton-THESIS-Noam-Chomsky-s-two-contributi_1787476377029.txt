THESIS: Noam Chomsky's two contributions to philosophy are the poverty of stimulus argument for pre-experiential (a priori) knowledge and his argument for sub-personal thought. The first establishes that innate cognitive structure, not experience, is the primary determinant of linguistic and other cognitive competence. The second establishes that this structure is not merely physiological but genuinely cognitive, and that it operates below the level of consciousness, constituting the subject of awareness rather than being an object of it.

OUTLINE:

I. The Poverty of Stimulus Argument
   A. The empirical datum: Humans acquire language from sparse, degraded data; animals and inanimate objects do not, even when exposed to identical stimuli.
   B. The inference: Humans must possess innate cognitive structure that organizes and interprets sensory input, turning mere stimulation into knowledge.
   C. The trigger metaphor: Experience does not create knowledge; it activates pre-existing cognitive structure. The structure "anticipates" or "prefigures" the knowledge acquired.
   D. Conclusion: This pre-experiential structure is cognitive, hence constitutes a priori knowledge — knowledge that is a precondition for experience rather than a product of it.

II. The Argument for Sub-Personal Thought
   A. The objection: Why posit unconscious cognitive structure? Why not reduce all such structure to physiology?
   B. The linguistic evidence: Language is productive (expressions combine into larger expressions) and systematic (parts of expressions recombine into new expressions).
   C. The inference: Productivity and systematicity of language reflect corresponding properties of the mind. We understand logical relations of dependence mapping sentence-parts to sentence-meanings.
   D. The physiology objection answered: Pure physiology yields training, not teaching; conditioning, not learning. The ability to handle novelty proves the mediating structures are cognitive, implemented by physiology as software is implemented by hardware.

III. The Nature of Deep Unconsciousness
   A. Distinction between knowledge one has (content of awareness) and knowledge that constitutes the framework of awareness.
   B. The hotel analogy: Cognitive structure is the hotel; mental contents (thoughts, feelings, perceptions) are its occupants. The structure is deduced from the occupants' movements but is not identical to them.
   C. The factory analogy: Thoughts are productions of the cognitive structure, not the structure itself.
   D. Self-awareness is awareness of products of the framework, not the framework itself. Hence the framework is "deeply unconscious" — not merely repressed (Freudian) but structurally inaccessible to introspection.

KEY TERMS WITH DEFINITIONS:

1. Poverty of stimulus: The fact that the linguistic data available to a child are insufficient, in quantity and quality, to explain the child's eventual linguistic competence. This poverty is the empirical basis for positing innate structure.

2. A priori knowledge: Knowledge that is pre-experiential; knowledge that is a precondition for having experiences of a certain kind, rather than being derived from those experiences. In Chomsky's framework, this is not knowledge the mind has but knowledge that constitutes the mind.

3. Trigger: The role of experience in relation to innate structure. Experience does not create or shape knowledge; it activates or "turns on" pre-existing cognitive structure.

4. Productivity: The property of language whereby expressions can be combined into larger expressions. "John loves Sally" and "Sally hates Fred" can combine into "John loves Sally and Sally hates Fred."

5. Systematicity: The property of language whereby the parts of expressions can be rearranged to form new expressions. "John loves Sally" yields "John hates Fred," "Sally hates John," etc.

6. Sub-personal thought: Cognitive processes that are not accessible to consciousness, not because they are repressed but because they constitute the framework within which consciousness operates. They are the subject of awareness, never its object.

7. Deep unconsciousness: Unconsciousness of cognitive structure itself, as opposed to the Freudian unconscious, which contains contents that are unconscious but could in principle become conscious. Deep unconscious contents cannot become conscious because they are the conditions of consciousness.

8. Competence vs. performance (implied): Competence is the underlying cognitive ability; performance is the deployment of that ability under real-world constraints (memory, attention, fatigue). The poverty of stimulus argument concerns competence, not performance.

9. Cognitive vs. physiological structure: Cognitive structure embodies understanding of logical relations and content-appropriateness. Physiological structure, considered purely as such, does not. Cognition is implemented by physiology but not reducible to it.

ASSERTS:

1. Humans, unlike animals and inanimate objects, acquire language from sparse data because of innate cognitive structure.

2. This innate structure is not merely physiological but genuinely cognitive — it embodies understanding of logical relations.

3. Experience plays a causal, not rational, role in knowledge acquisition. It triggers latent knowledge; it does not generate knowledge.

4. The productivity and systematicity of language are reflections of corresponding properties of the mind.

5. The cognitive framework that enables knowledge is not itself an object of knowledge. It is the subject to which objects are known.

6. Self-awareness is awareness of products of the cognitive framework, not of the framework itself.

7. The structures mediating language acquisition are "deeply unconscious" — not in the Freudian sense of repressed contents, but in the sense that they constitute the conditions of consciousness.

8. Where there is pure physiology, there can be training but not teaching; conditioning but not genuine learning.

9. Cognitive properties are implemented by physiology in the way software is implemented by hardware.

REJECTS:

1. Rejects empiricism (in the Quinean behaviorist form): The view that all knowledge derives from experience. This is "one of the most unempirical theories of all time," contradicted by the fact that humans learn language and dogs do not, given identical stimuli.

2. Rejects reduction of cognitive structure to physiology: If the mediating structures were purely physiological, we would respond to situations in "a few stereotyped ways" and could not keep pace with novelty.

3. Rejects the idea that the relevant unconscious contents are Freudian: Freudian contents are unconscious but accessible in principle; Chomskyan structures are deeply unconscious, inaccessible because they are the conditions of access.

4. Rejects the introspective objection: The fact that we are not aware of cognitive operations does not count against their existence. We are not aware of them because they are awareness itself.

5. Rejects the idea that innate knowledge is knowledge the mind has: It is not content within the framework; it is the framework. "Knowledge that you have is knowledge that presupposes an existing cognitive structure."

1. Assumes the reality of knowledge and cognitive competence as genuine phenomena to be explained, not explained away.

2. Assumes that the poverty of stimulus is a fact — that the data available to children are indeed insufficient to explain linguistic competence.

3. Assumes that introspection is not a reliable guide to the structure of cognition.

4. Assumes that logical relations (of dependence, of content-appropriateness) are real and that the mind grasps them.

5. Assumes that evolution can explain the existence of innate structure (though this is not developed in this document, it is implied by the broader framework).

6. Assumes a distinction between the subject and object of awareness, and that this distinction is fundamental.

7. Assumes that language is productive and systematic, and that these properties are not merely statistical regularities but reflect underlying rules.

8. Assumes that the mind is not a blank slate — that its structure prefigures the kinds of knowledge it can acquire.

14. The self: Not directly accessible to introspection. What we call "self-awareness" is awareness of products of the cognitive framework.

15. Logical relations: Relations of dependence mapping sentence-parts to sentence-meanings. The mind's grasp of these relations is what makes productivity and systematicity possible.
